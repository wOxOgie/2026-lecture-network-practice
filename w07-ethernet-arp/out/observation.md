# Week 7 · Observation

## Task 1 · A learning switch
- Never back out the arrival port: if A and C share port 0 (a hub or another switch behind it), C already heard the frame, and sending it back delivers a duplicate. Worse, between two switches a flooded broadcast would bounce A → B → A forever, and Ethernet has no TTL to stop it: a broadcast storm.
- A switch floods because "unknown" is bounded: the destination is somewhere in this one broadcast domain, flooding reaches it, and its reply teaches the switch (plug-and-play, no configuration). A router's "unknown" is the whole Internet, so flooding would multiply without limit; routers are configured or run a routing protocol, so no route means unreachable, and dropping is right.
- Aging uses time as a proxy for "still there". A station that moves or leaves silently keeps a stale entry for up to 300 s, so its frames go out the wrong port until it speaks or the entry expires. A quiet station that is still present gets forgotten and flooded to. More direct signals: flush a port's entries when its link goes down, a moved host announcing itself (gratuitous ARP), or STP's topology-change notice that shortens aging.

## Task 2 · Watch ARP happen (café Wi-Fi, me `172.30.1.62`, gateway `172.30.1.254`, `arp-capture.txt`)
- A2: the gateway `172.30.1.254` came back first, before my ping even ran (request at 23:13:28.842, reply 1.8 ms later). Every packet to an off-subnet address is framed to the default gateway's MAC, so background traffic re-resolved it right away. A3: `before` has 10 entries (gateway, two other café devices `.11` and `.94`, 7 static broadcast/multicast) and `after` has 3 (gateway + 2 multicast). I didn't talk to the neighbours again, and Windows recreates the multicast entries (MAC computed from the group IP, no ARP) only when it sends to those groups.
- A4: `www.korea.ac.kr` (`163.152.6.10`) is not in the table. ARP only resolves addresses on my own subnet. For anything else, my laptop sends to the gateway's MAC, and every router rewrites the link-layer header, so the server's MAC would mean nothing here. The table stays small however much of the Internet I talk to.
- B2/B3: the request went to `ff:ff:ff:ff:ff:ff` (broadcast: every station on the LAN receives it, because I don't know who owns the IP yet). The reply went to `e8:c8:29:2c:24:a0`, my own unicast MAC, so only I receive it. B4: 4 ARP frames in 70 s, all mine (plus a unicast refresh probe from the gateway at +24 s). On a campus segment of 1,000 hosts, ~1 broadcast/min each means ~1,000 broadcasts/min that every host must receive and process, ~10⁶ deliveries/min growing as N². That is why broadcast domains are split into subnets/VLANs.

## Task 3 · A switch that runs out of room
- Eviction: evict the entry heard of least often (LFU). Evidence: one counter per station seen, as source or destination (400 ints here, kept outside the 64-entry table; a real switch would use a fixed-size counting sketch with periodic halving). Result: wasted 156,998 vs 323,380, **51% cut (strong)**, 0 missed.
- Admission: yes. A new source is learned only if heard more often than the victim; otherwise it stays unknown. LRU alone cut 32%, and LRU + this admission test cut 51%, so declining is the big step. LFU + admission is 51.5% vs 51.1% without, close to a hindsight static top-64 (52.1%).
- R6: an attacker sends frames with thousands of random source MACs. An unconditional learner fills its table with them and evicts real stations, and from then on it floods their traffic out of every port, including the attacker's, so the switch acts like a hub and the attacker sniffs everything (MAC flooding / CAM overflow). Admission helps, since one-shot MACs can't displace heavy hitters, but the real fix is port security: cap learned MACs per port (e.g. 2) and block or shut the port when the cap is exceeded.
